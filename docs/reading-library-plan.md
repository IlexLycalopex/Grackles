# The library

The plan for capturing what is actually on the bookcase, getting it into the
database once and only once, and turning it into the thing the Reading List has
never had: a list of books you own but have not read.

Extraction is out of scope. Photographs of the shelves go through OCR and
enrichment somewhere else and come back as a file; this document starts at the
moment that file is uploaded and runs to the ongoing business of buying a book,
wanting a book, and getting rid of one.

Three things were asked for and they turn out to rest on one decision:

- capture the whole library from photographs, without typing it;
- search it quickly, so a book already owned goes onto the reading list in one
  press rather than being re-entered;
- have one row per book, because the shelves hold duplicates and the database
  should not.

The decision underneath all three is that **the library and the reading list are
two different things and want two different tables**. Everything below follows
from that, and the first section is the argument for it.

## The library is not the reading list

`rl_books` is a log. A row is one *reading*: it belongs to a year (`year_id`),
it holds a position in that year (`order_read`, unique per year), and it carries
the dates, the format it was read in, and the notes made about it. The year
pages, the target, the pace, `statsFor` and `yearProgress` all count rows in
this table and are correct to do so.

A book on the shelf is not a reading. It has no year, no position, no dates and
possibly no opinion attached to it — that is precisely what makes it interesting,
because the unread shelf is the thing this project exists to surface. Three ways
of forcing it into `rl_books` were considered:

- **An `owned` flag on `rl_books`.** Every owned-unread book then needs a
  `year_id` and an `order_read`, so eight hundred of them get filed under a
  year that did not happen. Every count on the site is then wrong, and the fix
  is a `where not owned` on each of them — which is the two-tables design,
  written as a predicate that somebody will forget.
- **A synthetic "shelf" year.** Same problem with a year number attached to it,
  plus a year pill in the nav that is not a year.
- **Reusing `coming_up`.** `coming_up` means *chosen for this year and not read
  yet*. Owning a book is not choosing it. Collapsing the two makes the planning
  year meaningless, and the planning year is a feature that already works.

So: a second table, `rl_library`, one row per book you own, and
`rl_books.library_id` pointing at it. This is the same shape the Cigar Lounge
already uses for `cl_cigars.reference_id` — the record somebody keeps and the
thing it refers to, joined, not merged.

Two consequences worth stating up front, because they are the payoff:

**A re-read is two log rows and one library row.** *Piranesi* read in 2021 and
again in 2024 is two readings of one book, and the library page can say so
without any of the deduplication below having to decide whether the second one
was a mistake.

**The log keeps what you read, not what you own.** When a library row is
created from a log row, or a log row from a library row, the fields are
**copied, not read through the join**. If the 2019 reading was a battered
Penguin paperback and the copy on the shelf today is a hardback replacement, the
2019 row should go on saying Penguin. A log that silently changed its own
history whenever a shelf entry was edited would be a worse record than the one
we have now.

### A naming collision to clear up first

The existing code calls `rl_books` "the shelf" in several places — `enrich.astro`
opens "Filling in the shelf", and the enrich route answers "Could not read the
shelf." Those sentences become actively misleading the day a shelf exists. The
wording to settle on:

| Word | Means |
| --- | --- |
| the reading list | `rl_books` — what was read, when, in what order |
| the library | `rl_library` — what is owned |
| the shelf | nothing, in prose. Too overloaded now to carry a meaning |

The rename is three strings and belongs in step 1, before anything is built on
top of the ambiguity.

## Identity: what counts as the same book

Everything downstream — the import's verdicts, the "you already own this"
warning, the unique index that makes one-row-per-book a property of the database
rather than a promise — needs one answer to *is this the same book as that one*.

The key is a fold of title and author, generated by a trigger on write:

    work_key = fold(title) [ + '#' + series_index ] + '|' + fold(author-surname)

`fold` is `normalise()` from `lib/title-match.ts` — NFD, strip combining marks,
lowercase, everything that is not a letter or digit becomes a space — so
*Bolaño* and *Bolano* are one book, and so are *2666* and *2666.*

Four rules on top of it, each of which exists because of a specific way this
goes wrong:

**ISBN is not the key.** An ISBN identifies an *edition*. The Penguin paperback
and the Everyman hardback of the same novel carry different ones, and would be
two rows — which is exactly the duplication being removed. An ISBN read off a
spine by OCR is also frequently wrong by a digit. ISBN is stored, is used to
*find* a book in a catalogue, and never decides identity.

**The volume number stays in the key.** `cleanTitle()` in `book-lookup.ts`
deliberately strips it — "Chew Vol 9 Chicken Tenders" becomes "Chew Chicken
Tenders" — because neither catalogue indexes a graphic novel under its volume
number. That is right for a *lookup* and catastrophic for a *key*: it collapses
an entire run of a series into one row, and this list has a lot of them. So the
volume is parsed out into `series_index` and appended to the key. The two
functions want different things from the same title and must not share an
implementation.

**The author is folded to a surname, and reversed names are unreversed.**
"Le Guin, Ursula K." and "Ursula K. Le Guin" are one author. A comma with no
conjunction after it means the name is reversed; a joint credit takes the first
name only, which is what `firstAuthor()` already does.

**A row with no author never auto-matches on title alone.** OCR loses the author
off a spine regularly, and *Blindness*, *Ghosts* and *The Trial* are each the
title of several unrelated books. No author means the row goes to a person.

### One fold, two implementations, and how they are kept honest

The key is computed by a trigger, for the same reason `publisher_normalised`
already is: a title corrected by hand on the edit page must update the key, and a
value the form is trusted to send is a value the form can forget to send. The
database is the authority.

TypeScript needs the same fold to *propose* matches in the import review before
anything is written. Two implementations of one rule will drift, so:

- they are pinned by a single fixture of pairs — same book / different book —
  that both a `node --test` case and a SQL test read;
- disagreement is designed to be cheap. If the fold in TypeScript misses a match
  the database would have made, the result is a row marked `new` that the unique
  index then refuses, and the reviewer sees a conflict. It is never a duplicate
  row, because the index is the thing that actually holds.

A `generated always as … stored` column was the alternative and is not usable:
the fold is not immutable enough to satisfy Postgres without pinning the
collation and the accent handling by hand, and changing the expression later
would rewrite the table.

## Schema

```sql
create table public.rl_library (
  id             uuid primary key default gen_random_uuid(),
  workspace_id   uuid not null references public.workspaces(id) on delete cascade,

  work_key       text not null,              -- set by trigger, never by a form
  title          text not null,
  author         text not null default '',
  series         text not null default '',
  series_index   numeric,

  format         text not null default 'print'
                 check (format in ('print','audio','graphic')),
  status         text not null default 'shelf'
                 check (status in ('shelf','wishlist','released')),

  year_published integer,
  pages          integer,
  publisher      text not null default '',
  publisher_normalised text not null default '',
  genre          text not null default '',
  tags           text[] not null default '{}',
  isbn           text not null default '',
  cover_url      text not null default '',
  description    text not null default '',
  notes          text not null default '',
  link_openlibrary text not null default '',

  source         text not null default 'manual'
                 check (source in ('manual','import','lookup','log')),
  source_batch_id uuid references public.rl_import_batches(id) on delete set null,
  source_photo   text not null default '',   -- which photograph it came off
  confidence     text not null default 'high'
                 check (confidence in ('high','low')),

  added_at       date not null default current_date,
  created_at     timestamptz not null default now(),
  updated_at     timestamptz not null default now(),

  unique (workspace_id, work_key)
);

alter table public.rl_books
  add column library_id uuid references public.rl_library(id) on delete set null;
```

`on delete set null` on the join, and it is the most important word in the
migration. Deleting a library row must never take a reading with it. A book
given away is a shelf that changed; the fact that it was read in 2019 is not.

`status` has three values for the same reasons the Cigar Lounge has three:
`shelf` is owned, `wishlist` is wanted, `released` is gone. A released book keeps
its row rather than being deleted, which buys two things — the reading history
keeps its link, and re-photographing the shelves next year does not re-import it
as a discovery.

`source_photo` is a plain text label, not a stored file. Photographs live outside
this tool by the terms of the project, but tracing a wrong row back to the
picture it came off is worth one column.

### The import, staged

```sql
create table public.rl_import_batches (
  id            uuid primary key default gen_random_uuid(),
  workspace_id  uuid not null references public.workspaces(id) on delete cascade,
  filename      text not null default '',
  content_hash  text not null,
  rows_total    integer not null default 0,
  rows_accepted integer not null default 0,
  status        text not null default 'review'
                check (status in ('review','applied','abandoned')),
  uploaded_by   uuid references public.profiles(id) on delete set null,
  created_at    timestamptz not null default now(),
  unique (workspace_id, content_hash)
);

create table public.rl_import_rows (
  id           uuid primary key default gen_random_uuid(),
  batch_id     uuid not null references public.rl_import_batches(id) on delete cascade,
  workspace_id uuid not null references public.workspaces(id) on delete cascade,
  position     integer not null,
  raw          jsonb not null,               -- the row exactly as uploaded
  title        text not null default '',
  author       text not null default '',
  work_key     text not null default '',
  verdict      text not null default 'new'
               check (verdict in ('new','duplicate_in_batch','on_shelf','in_log','ambiguous','unreadable')),
  match_library_id uuid references public.rl_library(id) on delete set null,
  match_book_id    uuid references public.rl_books(id) on delete set null,
  decision     text check (decision is null or decision in ('add','link','skip')),
  library_id   uuid references public.rl_library(id) on delete set null,
  created_at   timestamptz not null default now(),
  unique (batch_id, position)
);
```

Staging rather than writing straight through, for the reason the enrich batch
already gives: *a year half-enriched is worse than one never started*. An import
that half-lands leaves somebody working out which half, across eight hundred
books, with no record of what the file said. Here the file is parsed, judged and
shown before a single library row exists.

`raw` keeps every column of the upload, including ones this app does not
understand. Whatever the extraction pass found, it found once, from a photograph
that may not be taken again — dropping a column because the parser has no home
for it is the one unrecoverable mistake available in this whole design.

RLS on all three: the workspace's own members, `requireWrite` to upload or apply,
and no anonymous read. Nothing here is public.

## Uploading

`/reading/:workspace/import` takes a file or a paste. The parser is deliberately
forgiving, because the thing producing the file is a model in another tool and
will not produce the same headers twice.

**Accepted:** CSV or TSV with a header row, a JSON array of objects, or JSONL.
Delimiter and shape are sniffed, not configured.

**Required:** `title`, and nothing else. A file of bare titles is a valid import;
enrichment is what fills the rest in.

**Recognised headers**, matched case-insensitively with spaces, hyphens and
underscores equivalent:

| Field | Also accepted as |
| --- | --- |
| `title` | name, book |
| `author` | authors, by, writer |
| `series` | — |
| `series_index` | volume, vol, book number, # |
| `year_published` | year, published, first published, date |
| `pages` | page count, length |
| `publisher` | imprint |
| `isbn` | isbn13, isbn10, barcode |
| `genre` | category, subject |
| `tags` | keywords, shelves |
| `format` | binding, type |
| `source_photo` | photo, image, shelf, file |
| `notes` | comment, note |

Anything else is kept in `raw` and shown in the review as an unmapped column, so
an extraction that produced something useful under an unexpected name can be
mapped by adding one alias rather than by re-running the photographs.

**A file is imported once.** `content_hash` is unique per workspace, so
uploading the same file twice returns the existing batch instead of opening a
second. This is the same reasoning as the `idempotencyKey` on the enrich route,
applied to a form somebody will double-submit on a phone.

**Ceiling:** 2,000 rows per batch. Past that it is a paste that will time out
rather than an import.

## Deciding what is a duplicate

Parsing writes a verdict on every row. All of it is SQL and TypeScript; no model
is involved and the whole pass is free.

| Verdict | What it means | Default decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `unreadable` | No usable title | skip |
| `duplicate_in_batch` | Same `work_key` as an earlier row in this file — the second copy on the shelf, or the same spine photographed twice | skip |
| `on_shelf` | `work_key` already in `rl_library` — a top-up import finding what it already knows | skip |
| `in_log` | `work_key` matches one or more `rl_books` rows that have no `library_id` — you own a book you have already read | add **and** link |
| `ambiguous` | Titles are `looselyEqual` and the authors fold the same, but the keys differ | none — a person decides |
| `new` | None of the above | add |

`in_log` is the verdict that earns the whole design. A first import of an
established reading list will produce hundreds of them, and each one is a book
whose reading history and physical copy get joined up for free, in one pass,
because the fold was doing the work anyway.

`ambiguous` is where the near-misses go: a subtitle on one side and not the
other, a volume number OCR read as an 8 instead of a 3, a translator credited as
the author. It is deliberately a small pile. Two rows can only be ambiguous with
each other, never merged automatically, and the review shows both in full with
the fields that differ marked.

**Nothing merges silently across an author boundary**, and nothing merges on
title alone. The cost of a missed merge is one duplicate row that shows up on
the library page next to its twin and is merged by hand in one press. The cost
of a wrong merge is a book that has quietly become a different book, discovered
months later. They are not symmetrical, and every threshold here is set on the
side of the recoverable error.

## Applying

The review screen is a table of rows, grouped by verdict, with the default
decisions pre-selected. Ambiguous rows are pinned to the top because they are the
only ones that require attention; the other several hundred are correct and want
one press between them.

`rl_apply_import(p_batch uuid)` does the work in one transaction:

1. Refuses unless the batch is in `review` and the caller may write.
2. For each row decided `add`: insert into `rl_library`, taking `library_id`
   back onto the import row.
3. For each row decided `link` (and each `add` whose verdict was `in_log`):
   set `rl_books.library_id` on every matching log row.
4. `skip` writes nothing but keeps the row, so the batch remains a full account
   of what the file said and what was done about it.
5. Marks the batch `applied` and records `rows_accepted`.

One statement, because a partially applied import is the state with no good
next action. If the unique index refuses a row — the TypeScript fold and the SQL
fold disagreeing, which is the one way this happens — the whole apply rolls back
and the review says which row and why. That is the drift detector working, and
it is better than an import that silently dropped one book.

An applied batch is not deletable from the UI. It is how you find out where a
strange row came from.

## Filling the library in

Eight hundred rows carrying a title and an author is a list, not a library.
Covers, page counts, publishers, years and genre are what make it searchable and
browsable, and `reading.enrich` already does exactly this job for `rl_books`.

**Point it at `rl_library` rather than writing a second one.** The item ref
becomes `{ library_id }` or `{ book_id }` and the runner branches on which
arrived; the prompt, the OpenLibrary candidate fetch, the validator, the
proposal ledger, the budget envelope, the tick loop and the review screen are
untouched. One feature key, one prompt version history, one place where the
cost of enriching a book is recorded.

Two things do change, and both are about a spine being less than a book:

**The candidate set is wider.** A log row usually carries a publisher and a page
count to disambiguate with; an imported row often has title and author and
nothing else. So "none of these is the book" is a much more common correct
answer here, and the prompt already says so in those words. Rows that come back
unmatched stay thin and are listed on the library page as such — a book with no
cover is a book somebody can fix in ten seconds, and a wrong cover is one nobody
will ever notice.

**The free half runs first, and often finishes.** Where the import supplied an
ISBN, OpenLibrary answers exactly and no model call is needed at all. Where
title and author return a single candidate whose title and author both match
under `looselyEqual`, the same is true. The model is paid for only when there is
a choice to make or a genre to place — which is the cheapest-first rule the
cigar lookup already argues for, applied to a batch instead of a button.

**What it costs.** Roughly 700 prompt tokens and 120 completion tokens per book
that reaches the model, at the seeded $0.30/$1.20 per million — about $0.00035 a
book, so a full library of 800 lands near **$0.28** and comfortably inside the
$5 monthly default. These are estimates; the ledger replaces them with
measurements, which is the point of it.

**What leaves the machine.** `reading.enrich` is registered `sends_records`, so
the project must have consented, and what is actually sent is title, author,
publisher, existing genre and tags, plus up to three OpenLibrary candidates.
Notes, dates, ratings and the reading history are not in `EnrichContext` and must
not be added to it when the library joins — someone's private note about a book
is not needed to decide which edition it is.

## Searching, and the one press that matters

Four surfaces, and the important thing about the first three is that they cost
nothing.

**1. The library page — `/reading/:workspace/library`.** Filter box over the
loaded set, the same client-side matcher pattern as `CigarFilter`, with facets
for unread / read, format, genre, author and status. A cover-wall toggle,
because covers are being fetched anyway and a wall of them is how a person
actually browses their own shelves. Promote the matcher to a Postgres `ilike`
when the library passes about a thousand rows; it lives in a module both sides
import specifically so that day is a change of caller.

**2. The picker on the add-book form.** Type three letters at
`/reading/:workspace/book/new` and your own library filters underneath. Pressing
one prefills every field from the library row and sets `library_id`. This is the
thing that was asked for — *available in the database for me to search quickly
to add a new book to my reading list* — and it must never touch a model, never
hit the network, and never cost a penny. A book already owned should reach the
reading list faster than it could be typed.

Books already read carry a badge saying when, so a re-read is a deliberate
choice rather than a surprise.

**3. `Add to 2026`, from the library page.** The reverse direction: one press
creates the log row in the current or planning year at the next free position,
with `coming_up` ticked, fields copied across and `library_id` set. Building next
year's plan becomes picking from your own shelves, which is what the planning
year has wanted since it was built.

And the inverse for the books that predate all this: a log row with no
`library_id` gets an *I own this* button that mints the library row from it.
That is how the library keeps filling in between imports, at no cost.

**4. Ask the archive.** `library` joins `SOURCES` in `lib/ai/search.ts`, which
makes "which unread science fiction do I own" a plan the model can write:

    library — Books you own, on the shelf or wanted.
      title, author, genre, publisher_normalised, pages, year_published
      format, status enum(shelf|wishlist|released)
      isbn, notes, added_at date

The unread question needs one thing the vocabulary cannot express, because it is
a join rather than a column: `read boolean` — true when any log row points at
this library row. A generated view column, computed in the database, described
to the model as an ordinary boolean. It is the single most likely question to be
asked of this table and it is worth one view to be able to answer it.

No row is sent to the model by `platform.search` and that does not change here.

## Buying a book: the lookup

The remaining ask is the MiniMax path for a book that is not in the library
because it was bought five minutes ago. The Cigar Lounge's reference desk is the
model, and most of it ports directly.

Four stages, cheapest first, stopping at the first that answers:

**Stage 0 — your own records.** Free. Filters the loaded library and reading
list. This is also the *buy-guard*: standing in a bookshop, the first thing the
page tells you is whether you already own it. On the stated problem — a shelf
with duplicates on it — this is the highest-value screen in the document and the
cheapest to build.

**Stage 1 — the shared reference cache.** One select against a new
`rl_book_reference`, keyed on the canonical title + author fold. Global, not
workspace-scoped, insert-only, no update or delete, every row carrying who asked
for it — the `cl_cigar_reference` rules verbatim, and for the same reason: what
a book is, is a fact about the world, so the second person to look one up pays
nothing.

**Stage 2 — OpenLibrary, then Google Books.** Free, already built, already
tuned. `fillFromLookup()` in `book-lookup.ts` does this today and keeps doing it.

**Stage 3 — one call to M3.** Only on a genuine miss, only on a button press
that says what it does, never on a keystroke. `requireWrite` plus a per-workspace
daily cap of 50, which is far above ordinary use and far below anything alarming
— the cap is what makes the looser-than-owner-only gate defensible. Registered
as `reading.lookup`, `class: 'single'`, so it is metered like everything else.

### The model's answer is a better query, not a better record

This is the rule that decides what `reading.lookup` is asked for, and it is
narrower than the cigar lookup's for a reason: a cigar's dimensions are not in a
free catalogue and a book's are.

So the model is asked to *identify*: given "the new Rachel Cusk one" or a title
mangled by a phone keyboard, return the canonical title, the author, the series
and volume if any, and the approximate first-publication year — plus up to two
alternates as bare titles. The app then re-queries OpenLibrary with that, and
takes the facts from the catalogue.

What follows from that:

**It is never asked for an ISBN, and must not supply one.** M3 will produce a
well-formed, checksum-valid, entirely fictional ISBN with no signal that it did.
That number would then be written into a field that looks authoritative, used to
search a catalogue, and possibly typed into a shop. There is no column for one to
land in from this path — the ISBN on a library row comes from OpenLibrary or from
a barcode, never from a completion.

**It is never asked for ratings, review scores, sales figures, prizes or
attributed opinion**, in those words and with the reason attached, for exactly
the argument that dropped the Cigar Aficionado score: a fabricated citation to a
real publication is the worst output this app could produce.

**Page counts and publishers are catalogue facts.** If OpenLibrary and Google
Books both miss entirely, the model's title and author are kept — that is its
job — and every other field stays blank rather than being invented. A blank is
correct and fixable; a plausible wrong number is neither.

**Null is a correct answer and the prompt says so.** Same as the cigar lookup.

Reply shape:

```json
{
  "title": "Second Place",
  "author": "Rachel Cusk",
  "series": null,
  "series_index": null,
  "year_published": 2021,
  "confidence": "high",
  "alternates": ["Outline", "Parade"]
}
```

Estimated cost, uncached: about 350 prompt tokens and 90 completion — under a
twentieth of a penny, and most lookups never reach it.

### Where it lands

`/reading/:workspace/lookup` is the reference page, and a result becomes a URL
(`?reference=<id>`) rather than being handed across in a query string — the
shape the cigar desk arrived at when it was built, because it makes the page
shareable and the prefill server-side. From a result: *add to library*, *add to
wishlist*, or *add to library and to this year*.

The same panel appears on the add-book form, where it fills blanks only and
never overwrites something typed.

## Ongoing management

What the year after the import looks like.

**A book is bought.** Lookup → library. If it is going to be read next, one more
press puts it on the year as `coming_up`. Where the phone supports
`BarcodeDetector` — Chrome and Android do — pointing the camera at the barcode
resolves the ISBN and goes straight to OpenLibrary, which is exact, instant and
free. That path should be built before the model path is leaned on, because it
makes it rare.

**A book is wanted.** `status: wishlist`. The wishlist is a filter on the library
page, not a fourth table, and getting one off it is a single update — which is
what the Cigar Lounge's wishlist concluded after building it.

**A book leaves.** `status: released`, never a delete. Keeps the reading history
joined, keeps next year's import from rediscovering it, and keeps *what I used
to own* answerable.

**More shelves are photographed.** A second batch. The great majority of it
comes back `on_shelf` and is skipped in one press; the interesting rows are the
handful of new ones and the ambiguities. This is the case the whole staging
design is shaped around — the import is not a one-off migration, it is the way
the library is maintained.

**Something is wrong.** The library row has an edit page like everything else on
the site. Two rows that turn out to be one get a *merge* action: repoint every
`rl_books.library_id`, keep the older row's `added_at`, take the fuller value
field by field with the survivor winning ties, and delete the loser. The unique
index means this can only ever be a manual correction of a near-match, not a
bulk cleanup.

## Enhancements worth having

Ranked by what they give back for what they cost. The first four are effectively
free, because they are queries against a join that will already exist.

**1. The unread shelf.** `status = 'shelf' and not read`, sorted by when it was
acquired. This is the answer to the question that started the project and it is
one view. Everything else on this list is a variation of it.

**2. The buy-guard.** Stage 0 of the lookup, on a phone, in a shop. Free, and it
is the feature that stops the duplicates coming back.

**3. Shelf roulette.** Pick me something — random from the unread shelf, filtered
by mood: under 300 pages, fiction, something I have owned longest, an author I
have read before and liked. A tiny surface with a disproportionate effect on
whether the unread shelf gets read.

**4. The numbers.** How much of the library is unread, as a proportion and a
page count; the ten longest-owned unread books; authors of whom you own more
than one and have read none; how the year's reading split between books already
owned and books bought to read. Direct extensions of `statsFor`.

**5. Plan next year from the library.** The planning year already exists and
already counts differently from a live one. Let a plan be assembled by picking
from the unread shelf against the year's target, showing the running page count
against the pace `yearProgress` computes.

**6. Duplicate radar, continuous.** The import's ambiguity check, run as a
background query over the library rather than over a batch, surfacing near-twins
whenever they appear. Cheap insurance against the problem returning by a route
the import never sees.

**7. Export.** The library as CSV, from the app, on one button. There is a
`backups/` directory in this repo and this data was expensive to capture.

**8. The cover wall.** Sounds cosmetic, is not: eight hundred covers is the only
view of a library a person can actually scan, and it makes a missing cover
visible, which is the signal that enrichment failed on that row.

**9. Lending.** Deliberately near the bottom. One nullable `lent_to` and a date
is genuinely useful and is also the first step onto the road this project has
already said it is not taking. If it happens, it happens as two columns and no
screens.

## What this deliberately is not

- **Not copy tracking.** No quantity, no condition, no edition-per-copy. Two
  copies of the same book is one row; that was the ask.
- **Not shelf locations.** No room, no case, no position. `source_photo` records
  which photograph a row came off, which is provenance, not a map.
- **Not a valuation, an insurance schedule, or a catalogue for anybody else.**
- **Not a Goodreads or StoryGraph sync.** An import format that accepts a CSV
  will accept theirs, which is the whole of the integration worth having.
- **Not an automatic re-shelving audit.** Diffing this year's photographs against
  last year's to find books that have gone missing was considered and is
  deferred: it needs the photographs to be comparable in a way nothing here
  guarantees, and it answers a question about the shelves rather than about the
  reading.

## Order of work

Each step is useful on its own, and the first three deliver the stated ask
without any model being involved at all.

1. **Schema and the rename.** `rl_library`, the import tables, `library_id`, the
   `work_key` trigger and its fixture. Retire "the shelf" as a word for
   `rl_books`. Nothing user-facing; everything below rests on it.
2. **Import and review.** `/reading/:workspace/import`, the parser, the verdicts,
   `rl_apply_import`. At the end of this step the library exists and is joined to
   the reading history. **This is the milestone that matters** — everything after
   it is making the data easier to use.
3. **The library page and the picker.** Filter box, facets, cover wall, the
   one-press add in both directions. Free, and it is what the project was for.
4. **Enrichment.** Point `reading.enrich` at library rows; run it over the
   import; review the proposals on the page that already exists.
5. **The lookup.** `rl_book_reference`, `reading.lookup`, the four stages, the
   reference page and the panel. Barcode scanning first, because it makes stage 3
   rare.
6. **Ask the archive.** `library` in `SOURCES`, with the `read` boolean.
7. **The enhancements**, in the order above, as appetite allows.

## Open questions

- **What the extraction actually produces.** The header table above is a guess at
  a file nobody has made yet. The first real export should be read before the
  parser is finished, and the aliases adjusted to it — one round of that is worth
  more than any amount of speculative flexibility.
- **Whether ambiguity should ever be resolved by a model.** A batch of forty
  near-twins is forty small judgements, and the enrich feature already shows that
  a model with the right context is decent at *this is not the same book*. It is
  deliberately not in this plan: the free rules produce a small enough pile that
  a person can do it once, and a wrong automatic merge is the expensive error.
  Revisit if the pile turns out to be large.
- **Whether the reading list should stop copying fields.** The copy-not-join rule
  is argued above and is right for history. It does mean a typo fixed on the
  library row does not fix the log row, and at some point somebody will want a
  *propagate this correction* button. That is a button, not a schema change, and
  it can wait until it is asked for.
- **Where the audiobooks live.** They are owned, they are not on the bookcase,
  and no photograph will find them. The format column allows them; the import
  will not produce them. Probably a second import from wherever they are listed,
  which is the same parser and no new thinking.
- **Whether `released` should be visible by default.** It is honest and it is
  clutter. Currently: filtered out unless asked for, which is what the Cigar
  Lounge does with smoked entries on the humidor page.
